1 · Font at Large should match the system default

You are right that L is off-standard: controlFont and scaleLabelFont both return 15pt at large, where the macOS control default is 13pt. That 15 was not an accident though — the comment sitting directly above it says 13pt next to 20pt glyphs "reads as too small". So the font is compensating for the glyph, and moving the font alone moves the imbalance rather than fixing it.

The number that actually governs how the row reads is the glyph-box to text ratio:

glyphSizeglyphFontcontrolFontratio (box : text)Reads as
a. Today2016151.33balanced, but text is 2pt above system
b. Font only2016131.54system-correct text, glyphs now oversized beside it
c. Recommended1714131.31system-correct text, balance preserved

Note there are two glyph numbers, not one. glyphSize is the layout box the symbol sits in; glyphFont is the symbol's own point size. The two track together at every scale today (16/13 at medium = 0.81, 20/16 at large = 0.80), so a box of 17 wants a symbol of 14 to keep that relation. Changing only glyphSize would leave a 16pt symbol rattling in a 17pt box.

Look at the three rows rather than the arithmetic. The imbalance in (b) is the thing the original comment was complaining about, and it is easier to see than to argue about.

a. today  ·  glyph box 20, symbol 16, text 15  ·  ratio 1.33
b. font only  ·  glyph box 20, symbol 16, text 13  ·  ratio 1.54 — glyphs now dominate
c. recommended  ·  glyph box 17, symbol 14, text 13  ·  ratio 1.31
a. today  ·  20 / 16 / 15
c. recommended  ·  17 / 14 / 13
Recommendation: take (c). glyphSize 20→17, glyphFont 16→14, controlFont 15→13, scaleLabelFont 15→13. Medium and small are untouched. One consequence to price in: controls get ~3pt shorter, so the whole row gets shorter, and every control gets slightly narrower — which gives width back to the camera menu rather than taking it. controlSize stays .regular; it is what makes 13pt the native size in the first place.

2 · Vertical spacing — superseded, and reversed

This section's original proposal no longer stands

It argued for making the bar roomier: vertical padding from 11 up to 15, with the bottom optically corrected to 13. You have since asked for the bar to match Photo Booth's title bar, and Photo Booth's bar is considerably shorter than ours. Parity requires the opposite of what this section proposed.

The original is kept below rather than deleted, because the optical argument inside it — that the top and bottom gaps are different kinds of gap — survives the reversal and still applies at whatever height is chosen. Section 2b is the live one.

What the original said, and what still holds

At large the padding is 16pt horizontal against 11pt vertical. The asymmetry was read as pinched, and the proposal was 15 vertical, drawn below.

Symmetric padding will not look symmetric — this part still holds

The preview runs flush to the panel's bottom edge, so the two gaps are not the same kind of gap. The top gap sits between the row and the window's own edge, which is soft — there is nothing there. The bottom gap sits between the row and a hard, dark, high-contrast image edge, which visually pulls. Equal numbers therefore read bottom-heavy, and the fix is optical rather than arithmetic: give the bottom 1–2pt less than the top. This remains true at a Photo Booth-matched height, where the padding is smaller but the asymmetry between the two edges is unchanged.

today  ·  top 11 / bottom 11, against 16 horizontal
superseded proposal — symmetric  ·  top 15 / bottom 15, reads bottom-heavy
superseded proposal — optically corrected  ·  top 15 / bottom 13
Red hatching tints the button row's own vertical padding — above the buttons, and between the buttons and the top of the preview. There is deliberately no band under the preview: the preview runs flush to the panel's bottom edge and has no padding. The hatch is a tint over real layout, so these three panels genuinely differ in height; it is a measurement aid, not part of the design.

These three panels draw the control at 24pt, which is what I estimated before the screenshot was measured. Measurement puts the typical control at about 20pt and only the pin at about 24pt — see 2b. The comparison between the three is still valid, since all three share the same control; their absolute bar height is about 4pt too tall.

2b · Photo Booth parity — the live proposal

You asked for the L bar to match Photo Booth's title bar, comparing the two in one capture. I re-measured that capture by decoding the PNG rather than by eye, and the measurement changes the conclusion I was given, so the numbers below are mine and the working is shown.

Correction to the brief I was given: 43px is the pin, not a typical control

I was told our control is 43px, which is 86% of Photo Booth's entire title bar, and that therefore parity leaves under 2pt of padding. The 43px figure is real but it is the pin, which measures 6–7px taller than every other control in the row. Mirror, the L toggle, the mic and the timer all land at 36–37px.

Using the typical control instead: 36 of 50px is 72%, and exact parity leaves (50 − 36) / 2 = 7px of padding per side rather than 3.5px. That is still tight, but it is roughly twice as much room as the brief assumed, and it changes "not viable" into "viable but at the floor". The pin being taller than every other toggle is itself a defect — the row is meant to read as one control family — and it blocks parity on its own. It is filed to BACKLOG.md.

Turning pixels into points, and why two calibrations disagree

The capture's scale factor is not given, so point values have to be derived from an anchor. Anchoring on our own verticalPadding of 11pt, measured at 19–20px above and below the typical control, gives ≈1.77 px per pt. That calibration then independently predicts Photo Booth's 50px title bar at 28.2pt, which lands on the 28pt figure I recall as the macOS standard title bar height. Two independent anchors agreeing is the reason I am willing to use these numbers at all.

The constraint that decides between them

The macOS HIG gives a minimum control height of 22pt. Under this calibration our L controls are already about 20pt, i.e. already under it, and every parity candidate goes further under — 18pt for A, 16pt for B. These are click targets, not labels: nine of them, several of which start and stop a recording. I am not willing to present 16pt as safe. If the calibration is off and our controls are really 22–24pt today, then parity costs less than this says, which is another reason the 28pt title bar figure is worth confirming on a real Mac before anyone writes Swift.

Is height parity with a title bar the right goal at all?

Said once, then I build what you asked for. Photo Booth's title bar carries a text label and three traffic lights, none of which are aimed at. Ours carries nine interactive controls, two of which start a recording. A bar holding click targets has a floor that a bar holding a label does not, so a literal height match is not a like-for-like comparison. My read is that what reads well in that screenshot is the proportion — a slim band of chrome above a large image, chrome that does not compete with the content — and near parity at 1.14× delivers that proportion while keeping the controls clickable. That is why it is my pick. If you want the literal number, parity A is the one to take, and it is drawn above.

Recommendation: near parity — control 18pt, padding 7 top / 6 bottom (the optical correction from the superseded section still applies), bar ~32pt, 1.14× Photo Booth. It takes the bar down by a quarter, reads as the same kind of slim chrome, and keeps the controls above where parity A puts them. Independently of which candidate is chosen: fix the pin's height so it matches the other eight controls, because at any of these bar heights a control 4pt taller than its neighbours is the thing the eye lands on.

What this implies for M and S

You said everything shrinks proportionally, so the honest statement of the consequence is this. Near parity takes L's control from ~20pt to 18pt, a factor of 0.9, and its padding from 11 to 7, a factor of 0.64. Applying the same two factors:

Proportional arithmetic, not measurement. M and S were not in the screenshot; those rows are today's PanelScale values scaled by the same factors, nothing more.

Two consequences worth deciding before implementation rather than after:

  • The scales converge. If L's control drops to 18pt it sits close to M's, and L then differs from M mainly in panel width and preview size rather than in control size. That may be exactly what you want — one row design, three preview sizes — but it is a change in what S/M/L means, and it should be a decision rather than a side effect.
  • S may not survive it. S is already the scale where rowSpacing was cut to 8 and previewWidth widened to 380 just to leave the camera menu a usable width. Shrinking its controls further without re-checking that arithmetic is how the menu gets squeezed back to nothing. Apply the factors to L first, measure, then decide M and S rather than changing all three in one pass.

3 · The "nice rounded corner"

There are two different rounded things in that FaceTime shot and you did not say which one you meant, so both are covered. They are independent decisions and could be taken separately.

(a) The window corner

FaceTime's panel is a floating window with a large continuous radius on all four corners. Ours rounds only the bottom two, at 10pt, because the top edge is attached to the menu bar — the panel hangs off the status item rather than floating free.

today  ·  bottom corners only, ~10pt — top edge meets the menu bar
larger bottom radius  ·  14pt, bottom two only — achievable today
all four corners  ·  14pt — the FaceTime look. feasibility unverified

Unverified. I do not know whether the third one is achievable on a MenuBarExtra(.window) panel. The window is created by MenuBarExtra, not by us; its top edge is flush with the bottom of the menu bar, and rounding it would either cut into the menu bar or leave two small wedges of desktop showing above the panel's shoulders. It may also be that the system draws the panel's background and our corner radius only ever applies to our own content. This is a question for the Swift Engineer before anyone designs around it, and I have deliberately not assumed an answer. The second mock — a larger radius on the two corners we already round — is available regardless and captures most of the softness.

(b) The control corner

The other rounded thing in that shot is FaceTime's controls, which are fully round capsules rather than rounded rectangles. That is already what Option B proposes and it is drawn in option-b-semantic-capsules.html; the active pin, mirror and M capsules in every panel on this page are showing it. Nothing further is needed here, but it is worth knowing the two are separable: you can take the capsules without touching the window radius, or the reverse.

Recommendation: take the bottom-corner radius from 10pt to 14pt now, which is safe. Treat all-four-corners as a question to the Swift Engineer, not a design decision, and do not commit to it in a mock until it has an answer.

4 · Material and density — not asked for, but probably the real answer

You did not raise this, and I want to be clear it is my addition rather than your ask. But having looked at the two screenshots side by side, I think the gap between them is mostly not colour, size or corner radius. It is two other things.

Material: translucent versus flat

FaceTime's panel is a vibrancy material — the desktop blurs through it, and the band behind the camera name picks up a warm tint from the video sitting above it. Ours is flat opaque grey that looks the same on every wallpaper. Both panels below sit on the same desktop so the difference is visible.

today  ·  flat opaque windowBackgroundColor
vibrancy  ·  translucent material, desktop showing through, faint tint pulled off the preview
Browser approximation using backdrop-filter. The real thing is NSVisualEffectView with a .popover or .hudWindow material and is not a CSS blur — it also picks up desaturation and a noise layer that cannot be reproduced here.

Feasibility unverified. Whether we can put an NSVisualEffectView behind a MenuBarExtra(.window) panel, and whether the system already supplies one that our opaque background is painting over, is a Swift Engineer question. It is possible this is a one-line change and equally possible it is not available at all.

Density: nine bordered boxes versus none

This is the one I would push hardest. FaceTime's panel draws its rows with no containers at all — coloured glyphs and text sitting directly on the material. Our row draws nine outlined rectangles, and Option B does not change that: it fills the active ones, but every inactive control keeps its own 1pt outline and its own controlBackgroundColor fill. The result is that the row reads as a toolbar of buttons rather than as a set of controls.

The variant below removes the outline and the fill from everything inactive. Only an active control carries anything, and it carries a solid capsule.

today  ·  every control outlined and filled, active ones capsuled
borderless inactive  ·  outline and fill only on active controls
today
borderless inactive

What this costs, honestly. Three things, and they are not trivial:

  • It contradicts a rule the project has held deliberately. CLAUDE.md states that every control derives a constant 1pt outline from PanelScale "so an off toggle and an on toggle read as one control in two states". Removing the outline from the off state removes exactly that. Option B already strained this rule by changing the corner radius; this breaks it. It is a real decision, not a tidy-up.
  • Hit targets become invisible. A borderless glyph gives no hint of where it is clickable. The mitigation is a hover fill, which macOS toolbars do — but that is a state the current row does not have and would need designing and specifying.
  • The camera menu loses its affordance. Without a border it stops looking like a menu. It probably needs to keep its container even when everything else loses one, which makes the row less uniform, not more.
Recommendation: worth prototyping, not worth committing to from a mock. If you want the "Mac-native" feel in one change, this is the candidate with the highest ceiling and the highest cost. My honest read is that it will need a hover state designed alongside it to be usable, so it is a larger piece of work than the font and padding changes and should be sequenced after them.

Open questions for the Swift Engineer

Read-only feasibility questions. No design has been changed to accommodate a guessed answer.

  • Can a MenuBarExtra(.window) panel round all four corners, or does the top edge meeting the menu bar forbid it?
  • Can an NSVisualEffectView sit behind this panel's content, and is the system already providing a material that our opaque background is covering?
  • Is a standard macOS title bar 28pt? Every point value in section 2b leans on it, and I could not confirm it.
  • Why does the pin toggle render 6–7px taller than every other control in the row, when they share a controlSize?
  • Does splitting verticalPadding into top and bottom values disturb the PanelSizer frame arithmetic, given the panel height is computed rather than intrinsic?
  • Does shrinking glyphSize at large change the row's height enough to matter to the frame calculation, or does it just give the camera menu more width?
