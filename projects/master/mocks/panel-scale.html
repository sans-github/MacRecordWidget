Panel scale — S / M / L

Three sizes, chosen with three toggle buttons in the row. The scale is not a preview setting: a PanelScale enum drives the preview size, the glyph size, the fonts, the ControlSize, the row spacing, the padding, the corner radius and the picker width together, so the whole panel grows as one thing. Medium is the default and the choice persists under panelScale.

The control

SML

Three toggles, 2pt apart — the selected one fills with the accent colour

Three Toggles in an HStack(spacing: 2), each with the same button style, outline and ControlSize as every other control in the row. The selected one is on; the other two are off. Each binding ignores being switched off, because a panel always has a size — clicking the already-selected letter does nothing.

Why not a segmented control. Mutually exclusive selection from a small fixed set is what Picker with .pickerStyle(.segmented) is for, and that is the platform-native answer. It was tried and removed: the segmented style is an NSSegmentedControl, which ignores SwiftUI’s .font(), so its labels stayed at 13pt while everything around them grew to 15pt at large and shrank to 9pt at small. A size control that cannot itself be sized is worse than the wrong container, so the toggles won. The cost is recorded below.

The three sizes

FaceTime HD Camera▾ SML

Large, drawn at 50% — 960 × 540pt on a 1920pt visible frame, .regular controls

Large tracks the display rather than a number: it is half the width of NSScreen.main.visibleFrame, floored, and clamped to at least the medium width so the three sizes never fall out of order on a narrow display. Because the panel is flush to the right edge, large occupies exactly the right half of the screen and sits beside a window tiled to the left half without lapping over it.

Two separate text tokens rather than one: a letter fills its box more solidly than a glyph does, so at small the S/M/L letters need to be a point under the camera name to carry the same visual weight as the icons beside them. At medium and large the two agree.

The spacing values sit off the 4pt grid at several steps (10, 14, 18; 6, 11). They were tuned against the rendered control heights rather than derived from the grid, which is a defensible reason but leaves the panel outside the standard rhythm. Recorded, not resolved.

Where the control can be reached

The S/M/L buttons are mounted only while the camera toggle is on. The reasoning in the build is that the size being changed is mostly the size of the preview, so with the preview closed there is nothing to act on.

That is not quite what the scale does. It also sets the collapsed panel’s width, its control sizes, its spacing and its padding, and those persist. A user who selects small, turns the camera off and quits gets a small collapsed panel on the next launch with no visible way to change it back short of turning the camera on again. The setting outlives the control that sets it.

Row fit

Small is the tight case. A 320pt panel has to hold seven controls, six gaps of 10pt, 20pt of padding and a 12pt minimum spacer. The four icon buttons and the three letter buttons account for most of it, and the picker is the only element that can yield — it is maxWidth-capped rather than fixed, so it absorbs whatever is left.

On the page above the small picker is drawn at the space that remains, which is well under its 150pt cap and enough for roughly a word of the device name. Whether a real 320pt row lands there or clips has not been checked on hardware; the numbers say it will be close. Flagged for verification rather than asserted.

Accessibility

ConcernStatus
VoiceOver labelsPresent and spelled out: “Small panel size”, “Medium panel size”, “Large panel size”. A bare letter would say nothing.
Tooltips“Small panel” / “Medium panel” / “Large panel”.
GroupingThe container carries panelScalePicker, but VoiceOver reads three independent on/off buttons rather than one control with three options — the trait a segmented Picker would have given for free. This is the cost of the swap described above.
IdentifiersOnly the container has one. The three toggles have no accessibilityIdentifier, so a UI test has to reach them by label or by index.
Selection cueAccent fill, the same cue every other on toggle in the row uses. Not hue-dependent: the fill is a luminance change.
KeyboardThree separate Tab stops rather than one, with arrow keys doing nothing. A segmented control would be one stop with left/right arrows.
